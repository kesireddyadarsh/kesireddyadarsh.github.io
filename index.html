Elite Multi-Criteria Decision Making—Pareto Front Optimization

Elite Multi-Criteria Decision Making—Pareto Front Optimization

Optimization is all about finding the best possible outcome—whether that means making something as efficient, fast, or cost-effective as possible. When there’s more than one goal to consider (and those goals might conflict with each other), we use something called multi-objective optimization (MOO). There are many smart ways to approach this, often inspired by nature—like genetic algorithms or other evolutionary strategies. These techniques usually produce a set of good trade-off solutions, called a Pareto front. Now, if you want to pick just one "best" option from that set, that's where Multi-Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) comes in. The M-PF optimizer was designed to combine these two ideas: finding a range of good options, and then choosing the most suitable one. This project introduces an improved version of that optimizer, called eMPF (elite Multi-Criteria Decision Making–Pareto Front). It still uses evolutionary methods to search for solutions and builds a Pareto front, but it also adds smarter decision-making to rank those results more effectively. The goal was to strike a balance between exploring new possibilities and making the most of what’s already been found. When we tested eMPF against a few well-known optimizers—like the original M-PF, NSGA-II, and NSGA-III—it came out on top in most cases. It not only covered the search space well but also delivered better overall solutions.

Multi-Criteria Decision Making—Pareto Front Optimization

Multi-Criteria Decision Making—Pareto Front Optimization

When it comes to solving problems with multiple goals, optimization usually involves two main things: exploring different possible solutions and making the most of the best ones you find. Most methods try to do a bit of both. But in this work, the focus is more on exploiting the solution space—basically, zeroing in on the best options once you’ve found a solid set of candidates. To explore the solution space, the method uses a mix of evolutionary algorithms and Pareto fronts, which help map out a range of good trade-off solutions. Then, to narrow things down and choose the top performer, it brings in Multi-Criteria Decision Making (MCDM). What’s new here is the idea of using MCDM during the process—not just at the end—to guide the evolution of better solutions step by step. This new approach, called M-PF (Multi-Criteria Decision Making – Pareto Front), blends decision-making right into the optimization process. And when tested against some of the go-to methods in the field, like NSGA-II and NSGA-III, it showed promising results in simulations.

Path Planning Optimization

Applied advanced algorithms for efficient autonomous navigation.

Flocking is a task where a group of agents—like robots or drones—work together in unpredictable environments. The main goals are for each agent to reach its target, avoid crashing into things (including each other), and stick to a certain formation while doing it. Since there are multiple goals happening at once, this kind of problem fits well with multi-objective optimization. Traditionally, flocking has been handled by simply adding up all the objectives into one score. But in this work, for the first time, Pareto front optimization is used instead—allowing the system to handle multiple goals more intelligently and balance trade-offs in real time. To get agents (like rovers) to work together effectively, the approach uses a Cooperative Co-Evolutionary Algorithm (CCEA), which is great at both exploring new strategies and refining them. CCEA is combined with advanced optimization methods like NSGA-II and NSGA-III to help the agents flock successfully in uncertain conditions. A new reward system was also designed to guide the agents’ behavior. To test it, the flocking simulations were run in different types of environments—both open and enclosed. The performance of NSGA-II and NSGA-III was compared across various scenarios, including different numbers of objectives. In the end, the results show that these updated methods are effective at managing complex flocking behavior through smart, multi-objective decision-making.

Tiny LLAMA Model

Generated a tiny llama model from scratch

A small Llama-style decoder-only language model implemented and trained from scratch using PyTorch. The goal of this project is to understand the internal architecture and training process of modern Large Language Models rather than relying on a pretrained model. The model contains approximately 3.9 million trainable parameters and was trained locally on an Apple M4 MacBook Air using PyTorch MPS acceleration. The model implements several core components used in Llama-style transformer architectures: SentencePiece BPE tokenization Token embeddings RMSNorm Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE) Multi-head causal self-attention Causal attention masking SwiGLU feed-forward network Residual connections Stacked transformer blocks Autoregressive next-token prediction.
